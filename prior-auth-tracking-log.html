A prior authorization tracking log your team will actually keep

Most prior auth problems aren't denials. They're requests nobody followed up on, approvals nobody told the scheduler about, and auths that expired before the procedure date.

A tracking log fixes that, but only if it's short enough that people keep it current. Here's a version that works for most specialty practices.

The fields that matter

Suggested prior authorization log fields
FieldWhy it's there
Patient and orderName, MRN, CPT codes and ordering provider.
Payer and methodPortal, CoverMyMeds, phone or fax, so follow-up happens in the same channel.
Submitted dateStarts the clock for payer turnaround times.
StatusNot started, submitted, more info requested, peer-to-peer, approved, denied.
Next follow-up dateThe single most useful column. Nothing sits without one.
Auth number, units and valid datesWhat the scheduler and biller need once it's approved.
Scheduled dateLets you spot procedures booked outside the approval window.

A follow-up rhythm

Pick a fixed cadence and stick to it: for example, check every pending request every second business day, and any request for more information the day it arrives. Sort the log by next follow-up date each morning and work top to bottom.

The handoffs that break

  • Order to auth team. Use one EMR work queue or task type so orders don't arrive by hallway conversation.
  • Approval to scheduler. The approval note goes on the order, and the scheduler checks it before confirming the date.
  • Denial to physician. Denials with a peer-to-peer option need a same-day message to the physician with the deadline.

If the log lives in a spreadsheet, keep it inside your secured systems, never on a personal drive. Better still, use your EMR's order status fields so the log and the chart can't disagree.
